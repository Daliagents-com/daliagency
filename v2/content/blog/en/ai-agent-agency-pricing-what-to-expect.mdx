## Direct answer

Expect a paid discovery or audit first, then a fixed-price pilot for one workflow, then an optional monthly retainer once the agent is live. Typical market ranges in 2026: audits from roughly $2,000-15,000, single-workflow pilots from $10,000-50,000, and ops retainers from $1,500-10,000 per month. Cheap unlimited "agents everywhere" packages usually mean thin demos without gates, logging, or ownership.

## The three-stage commercial shape

Most credible agent agencies price in three stages.

1. **Discovery or audit.** Paid, short, fixed price. Output: a workflow map, a risk list, and a pilot proposal with a written acceptance test.
2. **Fixed-scope pilot.** One workflow, one acceptance test, named tools, human gates on risky actions. Fixed price, fixed timeline.
3. **Ops retainer.** Optional. Monitoring, evals, iteration, and support after the agent is live.

Everything else you will see - hourly consulting, platform licenses, outcome fees - is a variation on these three.

## Pricing models compared

| Model | What it optimizes | Where it backfires |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hourly consulting | Flexibility when scope is unclear | The meter runs while scope drifts |
| Fixed-price pilot | Clear scope, shared risk | Padding when the brief is vague |
| Monthly retainer | Ongoing ops and iteration | Standby billing with no defined SLA |
| Per-agent or per-seat license | Predictable vendor revenue | You pay for a platform you may not need |
| Outcome or revenue share | Aligned incentives on paper | Attribution disputes, complex contracts |

Prefer a fixed pilot for the build and a defined-scope retainer for operations. Prefer implementing inside tools you already pay for over new platform licenses, unless a new tool genuinely is the product.

## Typical market ranges

These are typical market ranges, not Dali quotes. They vary widely by region, vendor seniority, and how much integration work the scope hides. Treat them as orientation, not offers.

| Line item | Typical market range |
| --- | --- |
| Paid discovery or audit | $2,000-15,000, usually 1-3 weeks |
| Fixed-scope pilot, one workflow | $10,000-50,000, usually 3-8 weeks |
| Ops retainer after launch | $1,500-10,000 per month |
| Hourly consulting | $100-300 per hour |
| LLM usage, one moderate-volume workflow | Tens to low hundreds of dollars per month, scaling with volume |

A quote far below these ranges is not automatically a scam. It usually means something is missing: discovery, gates, logging, documentation, or anyone on call after launch.

## Cost categories buyers forget

The build is not the whole bill. Budget for these from day one.

- **LLM call costs.** Every agent run consumes tokens. Ask who pays the API bill, how usage is capped, and what happens when a retry loop misbehaves. Get a monthly estimate per workflow at your real volume.
- **Evaluation upkeep.** Models change and prompts drift. Eval sets need maintenance or quality degrades silently.
- **Monitoring and logging.** Someone has to read traces when the agent does something strange.
- **Incident response.** Who is on call, with what response time, at what price.
- **Change management.** Training the team, updating runbooks, widening or tightening gates as trust builds.
- **Post-launch support window.** A pilot without a support period is a demo with an invoice.

## What should be in a pilot quote

A production-grade pilot quote itemizes:

- The workflow map with the exact steps in scope
- Named integrations and who provides access
- A written acceptance test: inputs, expected outputs, pass threshold
- Gate rules: which actions need human approval, and who approves
- Logging and where traces live
- The handoff package: docs, runbook, credentials plan
- An LLM cost estimate and who pays it
- The support window after go-live
- Explicit exclusions

If three or more of these are missing, you are looking at a demo quote dressed as a pilot.

## Red flags in a quote

| What the quote says | What it usually means |
| --- | --- |
| "Unlimited agents" for a flat fee | Thin templates, no per-workflow discovery |
| No acceptance test | Success gets defined after the fact, by the vendor |
| No mention of human approvals | Autonomy by default, incidents later |
| Free pilot for production scope | Discovery skipped; the cost returns as rework |
| Platform license required before discovery | You are buying their product, not your outcome |
| No exclusions listed | Scope disputes, prepaid |

## Hidden costs of underpriced work

Underpriced agent work does not stay cheap. Typical follow-on bills:

- Rewrites after secrets end up in client-side code or logs
- CRM pollution from unattended automation writing bad records at scale
- Nobody on call when the agent misfires on a Friday night
- Knowledge leaving with the contractor, so every change starts from zero
- A second vendor paid to reverse-engineer the first vendor's system

The cost of one wrong autonomous action - a bad refund batch, a wrong email to a customer list, a corrupted pipeline - routinely exceeds the price gap between the cheap bid and the credible one.

## How to compare quotes fairly

1. **Normalize scope.** Put every quote against the same workflow, tools, volume, and acceptance test. If vendors interpreted your brief differently, fix the brief and re-ask.
2. **Ask what happens when the agent is wrong.** Gates, rollback, escalation path, on-call. A vendor without an answer is quoting a demo.
3. **Ask who pays for production hardening.** Logging, evals, security review: inside the price, or a change request later?
4. **Compare total first-year cost.** Build price plus retainer plus LLM usage plus your team's time, not the pilot line alone.
5. **Weight ownership.** A slightly pricier vendor that documents the system and trains your team is usually the cheaper one by month six.

## FAQ

### Is a free pilot a good sign?
Rarely for production systems. Free work usually skips discovery, acceptance tests, and gates - the parts that make an agent safe to run. You pay for them later as rework.

### Should pricing include tools?
Prefer implementing inside tools you already own unless a new tool is the product. A quote that starts with a platform license before any discovery is selling the platform, not your outcome.

### When is a retainer justified?
When agents are live and need monitoring, evals, and iteration. A retainer without a defined scope and response time is standby billing.

### Who should pay the LLM API bill?
Usually you, through your own API keys, with the vendor providing a usage estimate and caps. Vendor-held keys make costs opaque and make switching vendors harder.

### How do I budget the first year?
Add four lines: audit, pilot, retainer for the months after launch, and LLM usage at your real volume. The typical market ranges above give a starting envelope; your integrations and risk profile move it up or down.
