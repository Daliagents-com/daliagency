## Direct answer

Dali recommends keeping the first note short.

The goal is not to impress a studio.

The goal is to give it enough context to ask good scoping questions.

Brief an AI agent studio with one workflow, the systems it touches, typical volume, failure cases, and the exact definition of success.

If the task is still a deterministic form, queue, or lookup, do not force an agent brief.

[OpenAI's practical guide](https://openai.com/business/guides-and-resources/a-practical-guide-to-building-ai-agents/) is useful here because it frames agents as the right choice when rule-based approaches are no longer enough.

## Copy-paste email template

**Subject:** Pilot brief - {workflow}

**Body:**

- We want help with {workflow}.
- Today the workflow runs through {tools and systems}.
- The weekly or monthly volume is {volume}.
- The cases that always need a human are {exceptions}.
- The risky actions that must be gated are {risky actions}.
- The data sensitivity level is {public, internal, confidential, restricted}.
- The internal owner or approver is {name or role}.
- A successful pilot means {success definition}.
- We want to start by {timeline or first call date}.
- Our budget band is {optional budget band}.
- If your procurement process requires NDA before sharing sensitive examples, we can do that.

Dali recommends keeping the body short.

Dali's default is to keep the first-contact brief to one page.

The goal is to make the studio ask the right follow-up questions.

## What to attach

| Attachment | Why it helps |
| --- | --- |
| Redacted examples | Shows the real input and output shape without leaking secrets. |
| Field list | Lets the studio map structured data into the workflow. |
| Role map | Clarifies who approves what. |
| Current SOP or checklist | Shows the present process and where it breaks. |
| Volume snapshot | Helps the studio estimate capacity and model usage. |

If you have no clean attachments yet, send only the email body.

Do not stall the conversation waiting for a perfect packet.

## What to answer if they ask for more detail

Answer in the same order you wrote the brief.

Start with the workflow.

Then explain the systems, the exceptions, the risky actions, the data sensitivity level, and the named owner.

If the studio asks for examples, send only redacted examples that match the exact workflow you want to scope.

If the studio asks for a broader vision, keep that separate from the first brief so the first call stays narrow and useful.

## What not to send

- A wishlist of twenty agents.
- Secrets, tokens, or raw credentials.
- A vague "make us more efficient" note.
- Screenshots without context.
- A budgetless note if your procurement process needs a range.

If the studio cannot understand the workflow from what you sent, the brief is too vague.

## What to ask the studio to include

Treat the reply as useful when it includes:

- Clarifying questions about steps, tools, exceptions, and approvals.
- A pilot boundary that names one workflow.
- An acceptance test draft.
- A logging and monitoring outline.
- A handoff and support plan.
- A clear note on what is out of scope.

That is the difference between a real scoping conversation and a sales deck.

## FAQ

### Should I include budget?

Dali's default is to include a budget band when procurement needs one.

Do not force a fixed quote before scope is clear.

### Should I send NDA first?

If the examples are sensitive, and your procurement process requires NDA first, do that.

If your procurement process does not require an NDA and every attachment is safely redacted, you can send the brief first.

### Is this enough to get a price?

It is enough to ask whether the studio can provide a preliminary range or needs discovery first.

A fixed quote still needs scope, assumptions, and exclusions.

### Can I use this for an internal team?

Yes.

The same brief works for an internal kickoff, a vendor email, or a discovery call.
